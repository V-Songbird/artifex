# Output formats

A piece keeps one design box and one drawing function. [`core/render.js`](../../core/render.js) applies the selected playhead and scale without changing that design box. A piece that declares `boxes` can be drawn at another box in its ranges, chosen per export and recorded in the manifest as `size`; see [responsive boxes](../apis/piece-api.md#responsive-boxes). A film can also change its box as it plays, inside a frame of one size; the manifest then records the list of boxes as `reframe`, and `size` is the frame. See [box timeline](../apis/piece-api.md#box-timeline).

## Interactive page and PNG

`npm run page` bundles the registered examples into `out/index.html`. The page provides example selection, seed and parameter controls, playback, PNG export at 1x, 4x, and 8x, and a film: **MP4 1920 px**, with the long edge at 1920 pixels or more, or **MP4 1x draft**, at the design box, or **WebM film** where the browser cannot encode the MP4 film. PNG and MP4 export redraw into an offscreen canvas at the requested resolution.

Every control has an accessible name: **Seed** and **Playhead** are labels of their inputs, each parameter slider is named by its parameter and described by its meaning, and the SVG, MP4 and WebM buttons are each described by the note under them, which is a polite live region. After an MP4 or WebM export, that note (`#filmnote` or `#videonote`) announces one plain sentence of what was saved, then, when the MP4 was made twice, why: drawn again from its first frame in memory after the canvas lost its GPU or the video encoder failed, drawn twice after the encoder route failed and the film was converted instead, or its sound encoded again after its encoder failed, and then any warning. The colour route and timings of the MP4, and the frame gaps and timings of the WebM, appear beside it in `#filmfigures` and `#videofigures`, which are visible and never announced. Errors appear under the export buttons in an alert region. When a piece fails to build, the page adds that playback and exports are off until the piece builds. The picture is an image named by its piece, seed and frame, such as `readout, seed 7, frame 12 of 168`.

`npm run page -- ./piece.cjs` instead selects one external CommonJS piece and writes `piece-page.html` beside its source; [where output goes](command-line-tools.md#where-output-goes) gives the exceptions, a piece kept in a tracked library folder or at the top of `out/`. A style draws nothing, so `npm run page -- --style <name>` is refused with the paths of the style's guide and module. The page of a piece provides the same controls and exports. `npm run seeds -- ./piece.cjs 9 0.5` writes `piece-seeds.html` beside the source for comparison. See [external pieces](command-line-tools.md#external-pieces) for invocation from another project, bundled dependency limits and parameter sweeps.

Playing a piece that declares sound plays its soundtrack with the picture. The first play of each solve renders the soundtrack with `renderSound`, as the MP4 export does, and plays it with one `AudioBufferSourceNode` from the transport's playhead, plus the context's output latency so sound and picture reach the viewer together. The audio context is made, or woken, in the play click, because browsers start audio only in a user gesture; a piece without sound never makes one. Pausing, scrubbing, a new seed and selecting another piece stop the sound; a parameter change while playing stops it and plays the new solve's soundtrack from the playhead; and when the transport wraps at the end, the sound starts again with it. A lap of the transport lasts the frame grid, frames / hz, as the soundtrack and the film do, even where the declared duration is no whole number of frames. The transport's clock still decides every drawn frame: the audio clock is read only to start the sound. This is for listening while working; the MP4 export renders its own soundtrack and is unaffected. A soundtrack that fails to render shows its error and the picture keeps playing.

A failed build has no renderable frame or export recipe. The page clears stale artwork, keeps the original error visible, and disables playback and exports until the piece rebuilds successfully or another valid piece is selected. It does not export partially built geometry.

The rendering API accepts any positive finite scale, subject to the destination surface's resource limits. Higher resolution should preserve the composition. Increase only the detail whose purpose depends on output resolution.

The page defaults to **CPU reference**. Raster-only pieces with an explicit
`webgpu-pixels` preview descriptor also expose **GPU preview**, an approximate
opaque pixel renderer. PNG, MP4, WebM, contact sheets and reference recipes always
use the original CPU `draw`; GPU mode does not change exported pixels or SVG
eligibility. Native browser support and an eligible hardware adapter are required.
Unavailable adapters, software adapters, insufficient limits, shader errors,
timeouts and device loss fall back to the current CPU frame. The status explains
the fallback and the renderer control can retry it.

GPU preview is capped at 4096 pixels per axis and 8,294,400 pixels total. It uses
a separate GPU canvas and includes the copy to the visible canvas in preview
timing. Those limits apply to preview only. See the [piece API](../apis/piece-api.md#optional-webgpu-pixel-preview)
for the supported authoring ABI, lifetime and measurement limits.

The `pixel-field` example evaluates seeded value noise, domain warp and advection
per output pixel. Its CPU renderer allocates ImageData at the destination raster
size; increasing export resolution increases CPU work and allocation. Its shader
implements the same field with float32 arithmetic. This example has no vector
output and its eight-second timeline is not a seamless loop.

## SVG

`renderVector(piece, options)` requires the piece to declare vector output. The vector surface supports path geometry and refuses unsupported raster operations by name. A raster-only piece remains a valid piece.

An SVG has no pixels, so it cannot hold an image a piece declares. A piece that draws over an image and also declares vector output shows the image only under `if (g.canvas)`; its SVG then holds only the marks drawn over or from the image, and its PNG and film hold the image too. Its manifest still names the image. See [images](../apis/piece-api.md#images).

`npm run examples` writes SVG files for registered vector examples. The browser page also exposes SVG export when the selected piece declares it. Both embed the [replay manifest](#inspection-and-reproducibility), and for the same recipe the page's SVG button saves the bytes `renderVector` returns.

Ellipses and circles use native SVG `A` commands under a nonzero similarity transform: uniform scale with rotation or reflection. Reflection reverses the sweep; full circles use two arcs. Nonuniform scale, shear, singular transforms and radii that are zero or serialize as zero retain the cubic approximation. Coordinates and ellipse-axis rotation are rounded to four decimal places; nearly complete turns whose serialized endpoints coincide are split to keep them visible.

Identical input angles produce an empty sweep. Unequal whole-turn angles that normalize to zero retain a full traversal, matching the installed Edge comparison. SVG and Canvas antialiasing can differ for transformed ellipses. Stroke widths still use `sqrt(abs(det))`.

### Resolution-dependent detail in the inversion example

[`inversion.js`](../../examples/inversion.js) reflects circles in three disjoint circular mirrors using closed-form centers and radii, then emits arcs. Its depth-first traversal stops before drawing a circle whose diameter is below 1.4 output pixels. On native Canvas, it reads the current transform to account for PNG export scale; shared circles keep identical design coordinates and stroke widths as finer circles appear.

`VectorSurface.getTransform()` and the benchmark null surface return detached numeric `{a,b,c,d,e,f}` snapshots of the current affine transform. The snapshots are mutable, but editing one cannot change the surface; they have no `DOMMatrix` methods. Inversion uses this portable scale reader to add smaller circles at higher output scale without changing shared geometry. Surfaces without a reader use scale 1.

On the benchmark null surface, `save()` and `restore()` scope every style and the line dash as they do on `VectorSurface`, and `getLineDash()` returns the dash that `setLineDash()` set. Compositing, filter and shadow settings, which `VectorSurface` refuses, are kept on the null surface and scoped the same way. `reset()` on either surface empties the save stack and returns the transform, every style and the line dash to their defaults, as a canvas `reset()` does. On `VectorSurface` it also discards every mark, gradient and clip drawn before it, as a canvas `reset()` clears the bitmap, so the SVG shows what the canvas shows. The SVG still opens with the background rectangle when the `background` option is set, since that rectangle belongs to the export, not to the drawing.

Ordinary SVG export uses the identity transform and retains the design-resolution cutoff. Enlarging that saved SVG preserves its geometry rather than regenerating detail. The cutoff alone ends every chain, but how many reflections that takes depends on the gap between mirrors and on the scale, so no fixed depth is used. Instead, the traversal counts the circles it would draw and raises the radius floor above the cutoff, to the smallest value at which the tree fits 24,573 circles. When the cutoff already fits, the floor equals it. Every circle drawn at a lower scale is also above the floor at a higher one, so a higher scale only adds smaller circles.

## MP4

The page offers MP4 export for animated pieces at its default size and bitrate (see [size and bitrate](#size-and-bitrate)), and at 1x of the design box. [`core/film.js`](../../core/film.js) draws every frame of `playheads(piece)` into an offscreen canvas and hands it to a WebCodecs `VideoEncoder` with the timestamp `i / hz`. Nothing is paced by the wall clock: a piece that draws slower than its frame rate takes longer to export and still keeps every frame at its declared time. When the piece declares `sound`, its soundtrack is rendered offline, levelled to -14 LUFS (see [soundtrack loudness](#soundtrack-loudness)) and encoded as AAC, or as Opus where the browser's `AudioEncoder` refuses AAC. The soundtrack is made alongside the frames: its rendering and encoding run off the page's thread, each frame yields once to the soundtrack's steps on that thread while it is being made, and the file is written once both are done. A soundtrack that fails stops the frames at the next one and fails the export with its own message. The file is H.264 video in an MP4 container, with the soundtrack as a second track.

The exporter requires `VideoEncoder` and `VideoFrame`, and for a piece with sound `AudioEncoder`, `AudioData` and `OfflineAudioContext` with AAC or Opus encoding. A piece that declares sound in a browser that encodes neither is refused before any frame is drawn, rather than exported silent. It declares the lowest H.264 level that fits the frame size, rate and bitrate, trying High then Main profile, where High holds 1.25 times Main's maximum bitrate at each level, and rounds each side to the nearest even pixel count, at least 2, because 4:2:0 video needs one; an odd whole number of pixels rounds up.

AAC is tried first because more MP4 players play it. An Opus soundtrack follows the Opus-in-ISOBMFF encapsulation: an `Opus` sample entry at 48 kHz holding a `dOps` box converted from the encoder's OpusHead description (RFC 7845). The conversion keeps every field except PreSkip, writes them big-endian where OpusHead is little-endian, and sets the version to 0. Each packet's duration is read from its TOC byte. An encoder that describes no OpusHead fails the export, because nothing else records the pre-skip; it shows that only once it encodes, alongside the frames, which then stop. A player without Opus-in-MP4 support cannot play the soundtrack; check the player the film is delivered to.

Every film is limited-range BT.709. An encoder handed a canvas converts it to video colour its own way, and its choice of range can change between exports; platforms that re-encode uploads may mishandle a full-range tag. So every frame reaches the encoder as BT.709 limited-range video: luma in 16 to 235 and chroma in 16 to 240, BT.709 primaries, transfer and matrix, the canvas's sRGB-encoded values taken as BT.709's R'G'B', one chroma pair per 2x2 block from the block's average, and translucent pixels composited over black. The `colr` box always says primaries 1, transfer 1, matrix 1, full range off. The H.264 stream says the same, for a player or platform that reads the stream and ignores `colr`. Every sequence parameter set, in the encoder's `avcC` and any a sample repeats, gets a VUI video signal type: video format 5 (unspecified), full range off, and colour primaries, transfer characteristics and matrix coefficients 1 (ITU-T H.264, E.1.1). Every other field is copied bit for bit as the encoder wrote it, and an SPS without a VUI gets one that holds only the signal type. Edge's encoder writes a VUI without a signal type, which leaves such a player to infer limited range and guess the primaries, transfer and matrix. `readMp4` reports each SPS's colour description in the video track's `sps`.

### Colour routes and recovery

The encoder converts the frames itself where the export proves that it writes exactly this. In installed Edge, an encoder handed a canvas that the browser draws on the GPU writes limited-range BT.709 within a level of the conversion above, and reports that colour space; handed a canvas kept in memory, as `willReadFrequently` asks, it writes full range and reports that. The browser moves a canvas to memory by itself: three `getImageData` calls on one were enough, and from the next frame the film was full range, with a new keyframe and a new reported colour space. So the exporter hands the encoder a WebGL2 copy of each drawn frame, which stays on the GPU whatever happens to the canvas it copies, and reads nothing back. A test pattern of solid cells goes through the same copy and encoder before the first frame and after the last, and is decoded in software; every cell must be within two levels of the CPU conversion, and every colour space the encoder reports must say BT.709 primaries and matrix with limited range. The encoder must also return an output for every frame. Chromium's Media Foundation encoder drops a frame it is handed as a texture, with no output and no error, when it cannot take the texture's lock within 100 ms, which load can cause; it copies a frame handed over in memory, as the conversion does, without that lock. A film that fails any of these, or that the route cannot finish, is encoded again, converted as below. The route needs WebGL2, `VideoDecoder` with software decoding to I420, and `EncodedVideoChunk`. The test pattern is not part of the film, and the tag and the file check are the same on every route.

Otherwise, where WebGL2 is available, a shader converts a texture copy of the drawn frame and one readback returns both planes. Before an export uses it, it must reproduce the CPU conversion on a test pattern within one level; otherwise, or without WebGL2, the CPU converts each frame read back from the film's canvas. That canvas keeps one rasterizer for the whole export, so the browser never switches it part-way through a film. The canvas is asked to be read often (`willReadFrequently`), and before the first frame the exporter draws a cleared one-pixel canvas into it. In installed Edge, the frames then match those of a canvas the browser draws on the GPU rather than those of a canvas kept in memory, and the route runs about three times faster. So the browser is inferred to draw the whole film on the GPU and read each frame back; a browser without a GPU is expected to draw in memory as before. The exporter tests this on every CPU-route film. Before the first frame, it draws a small translucent test pattern on the film canvas, on a default canvas and on a canvas kept in memory, and reads each one back. It then clears the pattern from the film canvas and restores that canvas's state, so the frames do not change. `report.rasterizer` is `gpu` when the film canvas matches the default canvas and not the memory canvas, and `memory` in the reverse case. It is `null` when the pattern cannot tell, because the default and memory canvases match, as without a GPU, and on every other route or pass. A canvas drawn on the GPU can be lost when the GPU process crashes or resets. In installed Edge 154, a GPU process crash forced through the DevTools protocol (`Browser.crashGpuProcess`) made that canvas read back transparent with no error, and it fired `contextlost` only after the first blank frame. After the last frame, the exporter waits once more for a loss that has not yet been reported: in the page, where `MessageChannel` exists, that wait is one macrotask, because a timer is clamped to a second in a hidden page; elsewhere it is 100 ms. When the film canvas fires `contextlost` or reports its context lost, the exporter drops that pass and draws the whole film again on a canvas kept in memory, which a GPU crash cannot lose. That pass draws from a fresh solve of the same recipe, because the crash also blanks every canvas kept with the first solve. `report.contextLost` is `true` when that happened and `false` otherwise. The same crash can also stop an encoder: in installed Edge 154 it stopped the AAC audio encoder while the soundtrack was being encoded, and the export failed with `EncodingError: Encoding error.`. The exporter therefore replaces a failed encoder, but not always only once. The audio encoder gets one more try: when it reports an error, the exporter encodes the same soundtrack again with a new encoder, without rendering it again. The video encoder can get two more: a film has up to three passes, each with a new encoder, the encoder route, then the conversion route on the GPU or CPU, then a pass on a canvas kept in memory. When the encoder route is dropped, because its video encoder failed or for one of the reasons listed below, the exporter draws the whole film again on the conversion route. When the conversion route's video encoder reports an error, or, on the CPU conversion route only, its canvas loses its GPU, the exporter draws the whole film once more on a canvas kept in memory. On the GPU conversion route a lost GPU is not caught: it fails the export with `film: the GPU lost the colour conversion part-way through the film. Nothing was saved.` A crash that also stops the video encoder gives the outcome of whichever failure the export notices first. On the CPU route, that is `contextLost` true or a `video:` entry, and the film is drawn again in memory either way. On the GPU route, it is the failed export or a `video:` entry and a film drawn again in memory. This order is read from the code, not observed in a browser. A film can therefore fail on the encoder route and on the conversion route and still be exported from the third pass. If the second audio encoder or the in-memory pass's video encoder fails too, the export fails with that encoder's error and returns no report.

### Which failures the report names

`report.encoderError` is `null` when nothing below happened. Otherwise it joins one entry for each failure or dropped route with `; `, in the order they were recorded, and each entry starts with its cause:

- `encoder route: <reason>` when the encoder route was dropped after it had drawn and encoded the film's first frame. The film was then drawn again, converted on the GPU or CPU, and the film note says `Drawn twice`, unless that conversion pass was drawn again in memory too, which gives the in-memory sentence instead. The reason is the video encoder's error, `the encoder returned N of M frames`, `the encoder reported a colour space other than limited-range BT.709`, `the test pattern after the last frame did not come back as drawn`, or an error the route threw, as its name and message, such as `Error: film: the encoder stopped accepting frames …`.
- `encoder probe: <error>` when the video encoder failed on the encoder route's first probe, before the film's first frame was done. No frame of the film was lost: the film was drawn once, converted, unless that conversion pass was drawn again in memory, as a `video:` entry or `contextLost` true shows. This entry adds no sentence to the film note.
- `video: <error>` when the video encoder failed on the conversion route, so the film was drawn again in memory.
- `sound: <error>`, such as `sound: EncodingError: Encoding error.`, when the audio encoder failed and the soundtrack was encoded again.

The soundtrack is encoded alongside the frames, so a `sound:` entry can come before, between or after the video entries. A film that failed on the encoder route and then on the conversion route has an `encoder route:` entry followed by a `video:` entry.

A film was drawn again in memory when `report.contextLost` is `true`, which has no entry of its own, or when `encoderError` has a `video:` entry. An `encoder route:` entry on its own means the film was converted, not drawn in memory. A route that stops before it has finished its first frame leaves `encoderError` `null`, or names only an encoder error as `encoder probe:`: the route cannot start without a WebGL2 copy, `VideoDecoder` and `EncodedVideoChunk`, and its first probe can fail to come back as drawn, or report another colour space, without any entry. So `report.conversion` is the reliable sign that the encoder route did not make the film, and `encoderError` says why only when the route got as far as its first frame. The film drawn again in memory is not tested for where it was drawn, so its `report.rasterizer` is `null`. `report.rasterizerBeforeRedraw` keeps what the test pattern read on the CPU-route pass that the film replaced: `gpu`, `memory` or `null`. It is `null` when no pass was replaced, or when the replaced pass took another route. `report.conversion` names the route, `encoder`, `gpu` or `cpu`, and `report.convertMs` its time: on the GPU and CPU routes it includes waiting for each frame to finish drawing, and on the encoder route the two test patterns. Reading every frame back costs time, so in installed Edge the encoder route's whole exports, test patterns and soundtrack included, took less time than the GPU route's, and its decoded films were as close to the drawn frames.

### Dither

Limited range fits each channel's 256 levels into 220 levels of luma, so rounding every sample the same way merges about one level in seven, and a slow ramp decodes as flat steps up to twice as wide as the drawing's own. The GPU and CPU conversions therefore dither: before it rounds, each luma and chroma sample gets an offset under half a level either way, uniform and with a mean of zero. The offset is a fixed integer hash of the sample's column and row, each taken modulo 128, and its plane, so the same pattern lies under every frame of every film, two exports of one recipe still give the same bytes, and the shader and `rgbaToNV12` compute the same offsets. A value that is already a whole level, such as black, white or a primary's extreme chroma, stays that level. The encoder route has no dither: the encoder converts the canvas itself, from the 8-bit copy it is handed, and a 16-bit float copy, which could carry the offset, failed the route's range test in installed Edge 154, so the export fell back to the GPU conversion. The default films from installed Edge are therefore rounded as before. A PNG is not dithered either: the canvas stores 8 bits a channel and the PNG saves those pixels unchanged, so there is no finer value to dither.

H.264 keeps part of the dither at the export's bitrate. In installed Edge 154, on [`tests/fixtures/gradient-film.cjs`](../../tests/fixtures/gradient-film.cjs), three slow ramps at 1920 x 1080, the dithered GPU and CPU routes decoded with flat steps near the drawing's own width, where the undithered routes widened them. The dither costs bitrate on flat areas whose colour falls between two levels: readout's film grew about 2.4 times on the GPU and CPU routes. A film already full of grain, such as cues, does not grow. The conversion time did not change measurably.

### The film's replay manifest

Every film carries its replay manifest, so a saved film names the piece, seed and parameters that made it. The manifest is the solve's, as an SVG carries it, with `film: { frames, hz, loop, scale }` where an SVG has the playhead `t`: the number of frames drawn, the frame rate, whether the timeline loops, which puts frame `i` at playhead `i / frames` rather than `i / (frames - 1)`, and the export scale, because a piece may draw finer detail at a higher scale. It is stored in `moov/udta`, the user-data box, as a `uuid` box with extended type `8b2fd966-e923-4330-a28e-6d82587d1ec9`: the box header, the 16-byte extended type, then the manifest as JSON to the end of the box. The JSON is ASCII; any other character is written as a `\uXXXX` escape, so the bytes are also valid UTF-8. ISO/IEC 14496-12 reserves `uuid` for private box types and requires readers to skip box types they do not know, so a player ignores it; installed Edge decodes films that carry it. The box sits in `moov`, before the media data: it moves the chunk offsets and leaves every sample byte unchanged. The readout example's film spends 209 bytes on it. Tag editors and remuxing tools may drop it. `readMp4` returns it as `manifest`, or `null` for a file without one.

### The file check and the export report

Before returning a result, the exporter reads the finished bytes back: exactly one H.264 track, every declared frame, uniform frame durations on the frame grid, the requested size, a first keyframe, the limited-range BT.709 colour tag and the same colour description in every sequence parameter set, every sample inside the media data, the replay manifest equal to the one the export drew with, a movie and track headers lasting the film, and, with sound, one AAC track or one Opus track with its `dOps`, whose packets after its priming cover the whole film and run past it by less than two packets, and one edit that skips exactly the priming the encoder reported and lasts the film. A failed check displays an error and nothing is saved. A film refused for its frame count names the colour route that ran and the timestamps, in microseconds, of the first three frames its encoder returned nothing for. `window.__artifex.film({ scale, bitrate })` returns the report and blob without saving a file; both are optional, and `report.bitrate` names the bitrate the encoder was configured with; the report includes draw, conversion, encode, soundtrack and total times and the ratio to real time, where `soundMs` is the soundtrack's own time, which runs alongside the frames, and `encodeWaitMs` includes each frame's yield to it, `report.sound.codec` names the soundtrack's sample entry, `mp4a` or `Opus`, `report.sound` also gives the soundtrack's level and, more than 3 LU under -14 LUFS, by how many LU it falls short, as `short` (see [soundtrack loudness](#soundtrack-loudness)), and `report.manifest` is the manifest read from the file. The page's `film()` also adds `report.shots`, a copy of the exported solve's `state.cues.shots` as `{ name, start, end }` in frames. It is `null` when the piece keeps no list there, the list is empty, or any shot lacks a string name, starts before frame 0, ends no later than it starts, ends after the film's frame count or has a start or end that is not a whole number.

### The flash count

`report.flashes` counts the photosensitive flashes in the frames the film encodes, after the finish, as the [piece API](../apis/piece-api.md#the-exports-flash-count) defines them: `general` and `red`, the most flashes of each kind that any one area makes over the whole film, and `worst`, the one second holding the most flashes in one area, or `null` when no area flashes. `worst` gives `kind` (`general` or `red`), `start`, the second's start in seconds, `flashes`, and `x`, `y`, `w` and `h`, the area in design units. A film whose `worst.flashes` is more than 3 breaks WCAG 2.3.1; it is saved anyway, with a warning. The export shrinks each frame onto a canvas of its own and reads one second of shrunk frames back at a time, because every read waits for the browser to finish drawing; the film's own canvas is never read back. Where the browser has workers, as installed Edge does, each second of shrunk frames is handed to a worker, which reads it back and counts it while the export goes on drawing and encoding. Without workers, the page reads and counts it between frames. If the worker fails part-way, its count is lost, so the export draws every frame again and counts them on the page before it saves the film. `report.flashMs` is the time the export itself spent on the count: shrinking each frame, and waiting at the end for the last second to be counted. Without workers it also includes the reading and counting, and on the GPU and CPU colour routes some of the wait for each frame that `convertMs` otherwise includes.

After an MP4 export the page's note (`#filmnote`) reads those facts from the report: the frame count, size, length and bytes and the soundtrack's codec (AAC for `mp4a`, or Opus), then the sentences that say the film was drawn again or its sound encoded again, listed in [saving a film](command-line-tools.md#saving-a-film), and warnings. Beside it, `#filmfigures` gives where the colour was converted: by the encoder, whose range the export proved with test patterns, or on the `GPU` or `CPU`, with `report.convertMs`. For an Opus soundtrack it adds that the browser encodes no AAC, and that the film should be played where Opus in MP4 is supported, or it plays silent. For a film that flashes more than three times in a second, it ends with a warning that names the number of flashes, the second they start in, whether they are brightness or saturated red flashes, and the area. `npm run film` prints both, the note and the figures joined, warning included, on its summary line.

### What the browser check requires of a film

`npm run browser` exports a film this way in installed Edge for every example that declares sound, or for the first with a timeline when none does, and for the first that declares a `reframe` box timeline, whose film's manifest must name that list. For each film it requires the page to offer MP4 and hide WebM, and decodes the film. It then requires:

- the first, middle and last decoded frames to resemble their own drawn frames at least as closely as their neighbours (a held frame ties with an identical neighbour) and at 30 dB or better;
- a declared soundtrack to decode to exactly the film's length, within one sample;
- a fresh render, levelled in the page by gain and limiter, to report the export's `measured`, `gain`, `limited`, `lufs`, `dbtp` and `short`;
- the soundtrack to follow that levelled render for 1024 samples from its first sound, where a codec's priming would swallow or shift it, at 10 dB signal to error or better;
- the soundtrack to measure -14 LUFS within 0.1 once decoded, or, quieter than that, peak no more than 0.1 dB under the -2 dBTP ceiling for AAC or 0.15 dB for Opus, and to decode under -1 dBTP, 1 dB above the ceiling, in any case;
- two more renders of it to hold the same bits in every sample, since the examples sum their voices two at a time and Edge then renders them identically (see the [piece API](../apis/piece-api.md#soundtrack));
- the manifest, which it finds in the film's bytes with its own scan rather than `readMp4`, to equal the page's `manifest()` with the frame grid in place of the playhead.

It exercises the soundtrack codec the browser chooses, which is AAC wherever AAC encodes, as in installed Edge, and the colour conversion the browser supports, which is the encoder route there. Each film's report names it, and a film that falls back to the GPU or CPU conversion fails the check unless `--allow-fallback` accepts it on a machine without a usable GPU. Its summary line names, for every film, the soundtrack codec and the colour route.

The Opus path, the CPU conversion and the encoder route's refusals are checked in Node against controlled encoders. In installed Edge, forced runs check:

- the Opus path on every film with sound, by `npm run browser -- --force no-aac`, which refuses AAC so each film carries Opus, settle's and cues' limited soundtracks among them;
- the CPU conversion on one film, by `--force no-webgl2`, which hides WebGL2, so neither the encoder route's WebGL2 copy nor the GPU conversion is available and the CPU converts (see the [development guide](verification.md#what-verification-establishes));
- the WebM fallback, by `--force no-h264` (see the WebM section below);
- a lost GPU, by `--force gpu-crash`, which hides WebGL2 and crashes the GPU process of the check's own Edge once a third of the first pass is drawn, and fails unless the film reports its canvas lost and was drawn again in memory (see [the forced fallbacks](verification.md#forced-fallbacks)).

Every film a run exports is then replayed, and a film whose replay differs fails the run. A soundtrack whose decoded peak is not above 0.01 fails as silent. Without `--force`, the run also exports `tools/fixtures/flash-film.cjs`, which must count more than three general and more than three red flashes and warn, and replays it.

Decoded frames and sound are compared within the tested browser; they are not byte-identical to the drawing.

### Saving a film from the command line

`npm run film -- <piece> [--out file.mp4] [--seed n] [--scale n] [--bitrate bit/s]` saves the film the page's MP4 button saves; its other options (`--reframe`, `--force`, `--crash-gpu`, `--edge`, `--timeout-ms`, `--headed`) are in [saving a film](command-line-tools.md#saving-a-film). [`tools/film-cli.js`](../../tools/film-cli.js) builds the page of a registered example or an external module, opens it in installed Edge on the browser check's own lifecycle, selects the piece and calls `__artifex.film({ scale, bitrate })`, so the frames, soundtrack, levelling and replay manifest come from the page's one exporter. The command refuses a `--scale` or `--bitrate` that is not a positive number before Edge starts, such as `--scale must be a positive number, got 0`. It hands any other value to the page unchanged, and the page refuses one it cannot use, such as a bitrate that is not a whole number, with its own message. Without them the film takes the defaults below. `--seed` and `--reframe` are applied after the page selects the piece, so the film's replay manifest records them. The file's bytes come back from the page as base64 and are written only when all of them arrived. The film goes to `out/<piece>.mp4` in the library, beside an external module as `<stem>.mp4` (a module inside the library may write under `out/pieces/` instead; see [where output goes](command-line-tools.md#where-output-goes)); `--out` names another file, relative to npm's invocation directory, and must end in `.mp4`. With `--seed n` and no `--out`, the name ends in `-seed<n>`, such as `out/readout-seed7.mp4`, so a film at another seed never replaces the piece's own; `npm run still` names its file the same way. The export's report, the one the page reads from the finished file with its timings (`drawMs`, `convertMs`, `encodeWaitMs`, `soundMs`, `totalMs`), the soundtrack's `lufs`, with `limited` and `short` when the export gave them, the flash count `flashes`, `shots`, and with `--crash-gpu`, `gpuCrash`, is written beside it with `.json` added, and the command prints the page's own film note on one line. A film with sound also gets `<film>.mp4.sound.png`, a picture of the soundtrack the page decodes from the saved file; see [saving a film](command-line-tools.md#saving-a-film). `--force no-aac` or `--force no-webgl2` saves the film a browser without AAC or WebGL2 saves, an Opus soundtrack or colour converted on the CPU, and the report then names that condition in `forced`; see [saving a film](command-line-tools.md#saving-a-film). A film is judged with `npm run replay`, not by this command. The whole run has a ten-minute deadline by default; `--timeout-ms` takes up to an hour.

To cost a change to a piece's drawing or finish, compare `totalMs` between two films saved with the same piece, settings and colour route, not `drawMs`. `drawMs` times the piece's drawing calls, and the browser draws their pixels later, at the first step that waits for the canvas. When a frame reads its own pixels, as the finish's bloom does, the pixels drawn before that read are timed in `drawMs`. Otherwise they are timed mostly in `convertMs` on the GPU and CPU routes, and in `encodeWaitMs` on the encoder route. So a change that adds such a read can raise `drawMs` many times over while `totalMs` barely moves. Checked in installed Edge 154 on `cues` with a `finish.bloom` added, which `cues` does not declare itself.

### Size and bitrate

Unless the caller names a `scale`, the page draws a film with its long edge at 1920 pixels: `filmScale(piece)` in [`core/film.js`](../../core/film.js) is 1920 over the design box's longer side, and never less than 1, so a box already past 1920 pixels is not shrunk. A 960 x 540 box films at 1920 x 1080, scale 2; a 680 x 730 box at 1788 x 1920. The scale may be fractional; the manifest records it and replay draws at it. The page's **MP4 1920 px** control uses this scale and **MP4 1x draft** the design box. `__artifex.film({ scale })` takes any positive finite scale.

The default bitrate is 0.45 bit per pixel per frame, `width * height * hz * 0.45`, at least 2 Mbit/s and at most 80 Mbit/s: 22.4 Mbit/s at 1920 x 1080 and 24 Hz, 28 Mbit/s at 30 Hz. `__artifex.film({ bitrate })` replaces it with a whole number of bits per second above zero; anything else is refused by name before a frame is drawn. The encoder is asked for a variable bitrate, so a film spends less where its pictures are simple. The level declared holds the bitrate, so a higher bitrate can raise the level.

The default was chosen in installed Edge 153 on every registered example with a timeline, cues with its film finish, and `tests/fixtures/hatch-film.cjs`, a public fixture that shades a scene with ruled lines 3 design pixels apart, boiling on twos. The hatch fixture is the hardest of them: under 0.45 its decoded frames fell under replay's 30 dB floor, and above it a higher bitrate gained little for a larger file. At the lowest bitrates tried, its film also fell back from the encoder colour route to the GPU conversion. Cues' grain softens into mottling at low bitrates.

A film spends its budget only where its pictures need it, so a grainy or boiling film is large, and a simple one small. Edge can exceed the requested bitrate by up to a fifth on grainy films. Drawing at the default scale costs what the pixels cost: pixel-field, which computes every pixel on the CPU, exports at about 0.05 times real time at 1920 x 1080.

### Film length and the soundtrack's edit list

A film lasts exactly its frames, `frames / hz`. The movie counts time in the video track's own timescale, so the movie header, both track headers and the soundtrack's edit all state that length without rounding. A 2 s film's movie lasts 48000 ticks of 24000 per second.

An encoder's soundtrack does not start and end with the film. It opens with priming, samples the encoder needs to warm up, and its last packet runs past the film by up to one packet. Each soundtrack therefore carries one edit (`edts/elst`). Its `media_time` skips the priming, and its `segment_duration` plays exactly the film's length, with media rate 1. The encoded samples themselves are all kept.

For Opus, the priming is the pre-skip in the encoder's OpusHead, 312 samples (6.5 ms) from Edge's encoder. The Opus-in-ISOBMFF specification makes the edit list the trim and calls `dOps` PreSkip informative, so PreSkip is written as 0. Chromium trims by both the edit and PreSkip; a film that repeated the pre-skip in PreSkip would lose its first 6.5 ms there. A reader that trims only by PreSkip and ignores the edit list, contrary to the specification, plays the 312 warm-up samples, starting the sound 6.5 ms late.

For AAC, no decoder rebuilds the first half of a stream's first 1024-sample frame, and Edge's AAC encoder starts the soundtrack in that frame without reporting any priming: it stamps its first packet at zero. Encoded that way, the soundtrack's first ~500 samples decode to silence. So the exporter feeds the encoder 2112 samples of silence ahead of the soundtrack, the conventional AAC priming, and the edit's `media_time` skips them, together with any priming an encoder reports by stamping its first packet before zero. The lead is no whole number of packets on purpose: in Edge's `<video>` element, an edit that starts on a packet boundary starts that packet without the one before it, and the first ~500 samples are lost again. A player that ignores edit lists starts an AAC soundtrack 2112 samples late, 44 ms at 48 kHz; one that assumes the conventional 2112-sample priming without an edit list comes out exact.

In installed Edge 153, both `decodeAudioData` and a `<video>` element play an exported test film's soundtrack from its first sample to the film's last, AAC and Opus alike, and its first 128 samples decode as faithfully as the rest. Without the lead, both paths lost the first ~500 samples of an AAC soundtrack, at 48 and 44.1 kHz; the element's output stayed exactly zero up to sample 448, as it does for a bare ADTS stream. Firefox, Safari and FFmpeg-based players were not tested.

### Soundtrack loudness

Every film's soundtrack is levelled to -14 LUFS integrated loudness. Platforms that play films turn a loud upload down to about -14 LUFS and barely raise a quiet one, so a film mixed quiet would stay quiet beside everything else. A soundtrack whose peaks leave room gets one static gain. One whose true peak would pass -2 dBTP first, as a drum's or a voice's peaks stand above the body of a mix, has its peaks turned down by a look-ahead limiter and its gain raised until the limited soundtrack reaches -14 LUFS: the export then changes the soundtrack's dynamics. The limiter cuts by 12 dB at most, or by as little as 6 dB for a sparse soundtrack (see below). A soundtrack that would need more stops there, quieter than -14 LUFS, with its peaks at -2 dBTP.

A sparse soundtrack keeps its accents. When sound fills only part of a film, such as a crack or a lamp's click over a quiet bed, its loudness comes from the accents themselves. Limiting it to -14 LUFS turns each accent down toward the bed, which flattens the film's dynamics. The export therefore sets the limiter's depth from the share of the film's 400 ms loudness blocks that pass both loudness gates:

| Blocks that pass both gates | Deepest cut |
| --- | --- |
| 75% or fewer | 6 dB |
| between 75% and 95% | in a straight line between 6 and 12 dB, so two seeds either side of a threshold level alike |
| 95% or more | 12 dB |

Ten of the showcase site's seam films were measured, with their soundtracks rendered in installed Edge and levelled in Node. At 12 dB, five of them were cut 11 to 12 dB at their loudest accent, and only 60 to 76% of their blocks passed the gates. The drum and speech-like fixtures (described below) pass 99 to 100% of their blocks, so they still get the full 12 dB and reach the target as before.

Silence counts as part of the film wherever it falls, including a silent start or end. The crack seam's sound passes the gates in 90% of the blocks from its first sound to its last, but in only 60% of the whole film; leaving the silent ends out would cut it 10.6 dB again. The cost falls on a dense soundtrack that is silent for part of the film. Synthesized drums 8 s long with their last 2 s silent, or their first 2 s, are cut about 6.5 dB and end at -15.9 to -16.1 LUFS, where without the silence they reach -14. That is within 3 LU of the target, so the report gives no `short`. The drum fixture, `tests/fixtures/drum-film.cjs`, rendered in Edge with the same silent quarter ends lower, at -17.6 to -17.9 LUFS, and its report gives `short` of 3.6 to 3.9. Every registered example with sound was cut 4.41 dB at most, so the change does not reach them. With the 6 dB depth, the crack seam ends at -20.54 LUFS instead of -16.80, and the print seam at -17.36 instead of -14.20: these films play quieter than the films beside them, and the export report's `short` says by how much. The -14 LUFS target and the -2 dBTP ceiling are unchanged.

The ceiling is measured before encoding, and a codec moves true peak once it encodes, most where limiting has put many peaks at the ceiling: in installed Edge 153, at a -2 dBTP ceiling, limited drums and speech-like bursts decoded 0.14 to 0.71 dB above it, AAC and Opus alike, and at a -1 dBTP ceiling the drum fixture decoded at -0.66 dBTP as AAC and -0.61 as Opus. So the ceiling is -2 dBTP for both codecs, which kept every film below under -1 dBTP once decoded.

The limiter reads the soundtrack's true-peak envelope, the largest magnitude over both channels of the four-times oversampled signal described below, so one gain moves both channels and the stereo image holds. Every envelope point that would pass the ceiling is brought down to it on all twelve samples it is interpolated from. The gain falls linearly over the 5 ms before them, as the mean over 5 ms of the least gain needed in a window 5 ms and 12 samples long, which the limiter sees ahead of the peak, and it recovers exponentially with a 50 ms time constant; before the first sample the window holds the first sample's need, so a hit on the first sample is met too. The limited soundtrack's true peak is then measured, and a trim of the whole soundtrack takes up any excess, which in the tests is under 0.02 dB. The gain is found by the secant method from the gain the peaks leave room for, within 0.005 LU of -14 LUFS in at most eight tries, or stops at the limiter's depth. It is plain JavaScript on the rendered samples, so one browser levels a soundtrack to the same bits every time, in the export, in `npm run browser` and in `npm run replay`. In installed Edge, an 8 s film's soundtrack takes about a second to render, level and encode.

A limited soundtrack holds three arrays as long as itself, made once for all the tries: its true-peak envelope and the limiter's least need, 8 bytes a sample each, and the limited output, 4 bytes a sample in each channel. That is 24 bytes a stereo sample, three times the rendered soundtrack; true peak itself is measured 65536 points at a time, and a soundtrack whose peaks leave room is scaled in place. In installed Edge 153, levelling five minutes of limited stereo drums at 48 kHz allocated about 340 MiB and took about 11 s on one desktop machine; the time varies between runs and machines.

The soundtrack is measured as rendered, before encoding, to ITU-R BS.1770-4. Loudness is the mean square of the K-weighted signal, a high shelf of about +4 dB above 1.5 kHz and then a high-pass near 38 Hz, over 400 ms blocks that overlap by 75%, gated at -70 LUFS and then 10 LU below the mean of the blocks that passed. Every mean is of power, and a mono or stereo channel is weighted 1. True peak is the largest magnitude after four-times oversampling with the standard's Annex 2 filter. [`core/film.js`](../../core/film.js) exports `measureLoudness(buffer)` and `normalizeLoudness(buffer, codec)` for an AudioBuffer-shaped soundtrack, which levels it in place, gain and limiter, and returns the report below; `codec` is the sample entry the soundtrack is encoded in, `'mp4a'` or `'Opus'`, and any other value is refused. The page exposes the meter as `window.__artifex.loudness` and the levelling as `window.__artifex.level`. The Node tests hold the K-weighting to the standard's 48 kHz coefficients, the gating to EBU Tech 3341's cases within 0.1 LU, and true peak between samples to its +0.2 / -0.4 dB, and the limiter to meeting each peak ahead of it, on both channels and from the first sample, with its deepest cut exactly what the loudest peak needs, and to its 50 ms release, and the levelling to fixed bits on drums, speech-like bursts and clicks, to cutting sparse cracks over a quiet bed by 6 dB at most, to counting a silent start or end as unfilled, and to making no other array as long as the soundtrack than the three above.

The report also says where the limiter cut and which events still stand out. With `limited`, `sound.beats` gives each beat of the built state's `cues.beats` its span, `from` and `to` in seconds, and its own deepest cut `limited` in dB; a piece with no beat sheet gets `sound.blocks`, the same for every 400 ms block from the start. Each row of `cues.sounds` is an event at its frame: `sound.events` gives its `at` and its `prominence`, its loudest 10 ms of mean power in the 50 ms from its time over the median of those windows in the 300 ms before it, measured on the soundtrack as levelled, so a bed the limiter ducked counts as ducked. `sound.buried` counts the events under 6 dB over that bed, and `sound.close` those less than 100 ms after the one before, which land as one. A piece that keeps no `cues.sounds` gets no `events`; its `--sound` strip measures the events it finds in the soundtrack instead.

A soundtrack with no block above -70 LUFS has no loudness to set: it keeps its level, and the report gives its loudness as `null`. A soundtrack holding a sample that is not a finite number is refused, and no film is saved.

`report.sound.measured` gives `lufs` and `dbtp` as rendered, `report.sound.gain` the static gain in dB, `report.sound.limited`, only when the limiter cut, the deepest it cut in dB, and `report.sound.lufs` and `report.sound.dbtp` the level the soundtrack is encoded at. A soundtrack encoded more than 3 LU under -14 LUFS also gets `report.sound.short`, how many LU under -14 LUFS it ends, to two decimals; one nearer the target, or with no loudness, gets no `short`. `npm run browser` requires `short` exactly then and carries it and `limited` into each film's sound report. A film that falls that far short has peaks that would need more limiting than its depth allows, 12 dB or as little as 6 dB for a sparse soundtrack, so it plays quieter than the films beside it until the peaks are tamed.

In installed Edge 153 this was checked on the examples' films and on two public fixtures, as AAC and as Opus:

- [`tests/fixtures/drum-film.cjs`](../../tests/fixtures/drum-film.cjs): four bars of a kick with a click on its attack, a snare, hi-hats and a quiet bass. It needs the full 12 dB of limiting and ends just short of -14 LUFS.
- [`tests/fixtures/speech-film.cjs`](../../tests/fixtures/speech-film.cjs): syllables of a two-formant voice, some opened by a loud plosive burst, over a quiet bed.

Encoding at 192 kbps moved loudness by at most 0.05 LU, and all ten films replayed as matches. With one static gain at a -1 dBTP ceiling instead of the limiter, both fixtures would have played about 7 LU quieter.

### Joining scene films

A film of many minutes is too large to draw and sound in one browser tab, so make it scene by scene and join the scene films with `npm run stitch -- a.mp4 b.mp4 ... -o film.mp4`. [`tools/stitch.js`](../../tools/stitch.js) copies their encoded samples into one MP4 with `muxMp4` from [`core/film.js`](../../core/film.js). Nothing is encoded again, so the join takes seconds and loses no quality. The command line is described in [joining films](command-line-tools.md#joining-films).

Only films that Artifex exported can be joined, and all of them must share one decoder. Each film is first checked as the export checks it (see [MP4](#mp4)). Then each is compared with the first film. The command refuses a film, naming it and the difference, when it has:

- another size or frame rate;
- another H.264 configuration: a different `avc1` sample entry, which another bitrate can cause;
- a soundtrack where the first has none, or the reverse;
- another soundtrack codec or configuration, such as Opus beside AAC, or another audio bitrate.

A film without a replay manifest, and a film that is already joined, are refused too. Join the scene films of a joined film in one run instead.

**Pictures.** Every scene starts on a keyframe, so its frames follow the scene before on the same frame grid, with no frame added or lost.

**Sound.** The joined film has one soundtrack with one edit. The edit skips the first scene's priming and lasts the whole film. A later scene's priming is removed differently: its whole packets of priming are dropped, and the scene before it ends on a packet boundary. So each scene's sound starts on its first frame or up to one packet later, 21.3 ms for AAC or 20 ms for Opus, and never earlier. A scene whose start falls on a whole number of packets from the film's start sounds exactly on its first frame; with Opus's 20 ms packets, that is every scene start on a multiple of 20 ms. `npm run stitch` prints how late each scene's sound starts.

A codec decodes each packet together with the packet before it. So at every join, the first packet of the new scene mixes in the end of the scene before, like a crossfade one packet long, and the scene before loses up to one packet at its end. In installed Edge 153, an AAC scene decoded to the same samples as in its own film from its second packet on. Opus also predicts each packet's loudness per frequency band from the packets before it, so in installed Edge 153 an Opus scene of speech after a drum scene decoded further from its render over its first 100 ms, and as in its own film from 200 ms on. If a join must be clean, place it where the sound is quiet.

**Levels.** Each scene keeps the level its export gave it: -14 LUFS, limited to a -2 dBTP ceiling (see [soundtrack loudness](#soundtrack-loudness)). The joined soundtrack is not measured or levelled again. It is about -14 LUFS when every scene is, and its true peak is the loudest scene's. The crossfade at a join adds two scenes' sound for one packet, so a join between two loud scenes could peak higher; no measured join did.

**Recipes.** The joined film's replay manifest, in the same `uuid` box, is `{ scenes: [...] }`. Each scene lists:

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `recipe` | the scene film's own manifest, unchanged |
| `frames` | `[from, to)`, the scene's frames in the joined film |
| `sound.at` | the sample of the joined soundtrack, as a player hears it, where the scene's own sample 0 lands |
| `sound.judged` | `[from, to)`, the scene's own samples that the join leaves as the scene decoded them: past its first packet after a join for AAC, past its tenth for Opus, and before the next scene's first packet |

`npm run replay` checks each scene of a joined film against its own recipe; see [replaying a saved file](#replaying-a-saved-file).

Joined files use 32-bit box sizes and chunk offsets, like every Artifex film, so `npm run stitch` refuses films holding more than about 4 GiB of media together. It does not load the films into memory. It reads each scene's samples from disk as it writes them to the joined film, so its memory grows with the number of samples, not with the size of the films. Eight 8-second scenes at 1920 x 1080, 215 MB in all, joined with a peak of 83 MB of memory. The joined film is written beside the `-o` file with `.part` added to its name, and it takes the `-o` name only after it passes the file check.

In installed Edge 153, AAC and Opus films joined from three to five scenes, one of them a length that is no whole number of packets, replayed with every scene a match. With one scene's seed changed in the joined manifest, replay named that scene as different.

### Which film the page offers

For an animated piece, the page asks `VideoEncoder.isConfigSupported` about the H.264 configuration the export uses at its default scale. `filmConfig(piece, VideoEncoder, { scale })` in [`core/film.js`](../../core/film.js) makes that choice for both the question and the export. The page asks once per design size and frame rate. It starts and draws without waiting for the answer, and shows the MP4 controls until the answer arrives. Where the encoder accepts no configuration, or the browser has no `VideoEncoder` or `VideoFrame`, the page hides the MP4 controls and offers the WebM export instead. A still offers no film. Either film control stays disabled until the piece has a valid build.

For a piece that declares sound, the page also asks `AudioEncoder.isConfigSupported` about the soundtrack configuration the export uses: AAC, then Opus, at 48 kHz stereo and 192 kbit/s. `soundConfig(AudioEncoder)` in [`core/film.js`](../../core/film.js) makes that choice for both the question and the export, and the page asks it once. The MP4 export never writes a film without its declared soundtrack. So where the browser encodes the H.264 but neither AAC nor Opus, or has no `AudioEncoder` or `AudioData`, the page hides the MP4 controls and offers the WebM export with a note that the film is silent. A piece without sound is never asked about, so it keeps its MP4. Neither question holds up start-up or drawing.

`window.__artifex.filmFormat()` resolves to `'mp4'`, `'webm'` or `null` (a still) once the page has applied the answers for the selected piece. `window.__artifex.filmOffer()` resolves to `{ format, reason }` from the same decision, the one the WebM note is written from. The reason is `'h264'` where the H.264 film cannot be encoded, even if the soundtrack could not be either; `'soundtrack'` where it can but neither AAC nor Opus encodes the declared soundtrack; `'still'` for a still; and `null` with the MP4. A script can tell the silent case apart without reading the note. The scripted exports do not follow the controls: `film()` still refuses by name where H.264 cannot encode the film or neither AAC nor Opus encodes its declared soundtrack, and `video()` still records wherever its recorder APIs exist, so existing scripts keep working. An MP4 at a larger scale or bitrate can still be refused where the default encodes; its error names the size, and the bitrate when one was given, and asks for a smaller one.

## WebM

WebM is the fallback film: the page offers it only for an animated piece whose MP4 this browser cannot encode (see [which film the page offers](#which-film-the-page-offers)). The implementation requires `MediaStreamTrackGenerator`, `VideoFrame`, and `MediaRecorder` with VP8 WebM support. Availability must be checked in the browser used for delivery. It carries no soundtrack, even for a piece that declares one.

The exporter walks the piece's frame grid and paces frames at its declared rate, because `MediaRecorder` stamps frames by the wall clock. A piece whose frames take longer than their budget to draw and encode therefore cannot be recorded; the export measures how far it fell behind its schedule and says so, naming MP4 from a browser that encodes H.264 as the export that keeps every frame.

The recorder can also fall behind on its own: its encoder can take tens of milliseconds to accept a frame, most often its first ones. The export times each frame's drawing, with the read-back that counts its flashes, and each hand-off to the recorder, and the refusal names which ran late. It blames drawing when a frame took longer to draw and read back than its budget, or longer than the slowest hand-off; otherwise it says the recorder ran late and suggests recording again. The report gives the slowest of each as `worstDrawMs` and `worstWriteMs`, beside `worstLagMs`. Other work on the same machine can make a light piece miss its schedule too.

The first recording in a page starts the recorder's encoder first: Edge's encoder takes about half a second to return its first frame, and a film recorded while it starts loses frames after its eleventh. So once per page, before the film, the exporter hands a throwaway recording a blank frame every 40 ms until one comes back encoded, for at most two seconds.

The exporter asks the recorder for the data it holds after each warm-up frame and while it waits for the film's last frames. A browser answers each request with a `dataavailable` event. If no answer arrives within 10 seconds, the export fails and names the wait, instead of waiting for ever. The exporter also stops each recording, the warm-up's and the film's, and waits for the recorder's `stop` event. If that event does not arrive within 10 seconds, the export fails and names which recording did not stop.

Rendering and encoding are measured separately. Before returning a result, it parses the recorded WebM blocks and validates frame count and spacing. A rejected export displays an error instead of downloading a result.

`MediaRecorder` writes a live WebM. In installed Edge its Segment has an unknown size and its Info Duration is one TimecodeScale unit, so a player reports 0.001 s and cannot seek. Once the recorded file passes its checks, the exporter writes the film's length, `frames / hz`, over that Duration in the file's own units. A file with no Duration gets one at the end of Info, and Info grows by its eleven bytes. Every other byte is the recorder's, apart from the stored positions an insertion moves. In installed Edge 153, a recorded 144-frame film at 24 Hz reported 0.001 s and a seek to its middle frame stopped at 2.25 s; with the Duration written it reported 6 s and seeks to its first, middle and last frames each showed the frame asked for.

A WebM stores some positions as byte offsets from the start of its Segment's data. When Edge's recorder stops, it writes Cues after the last Cluster, and each CuePoint stores the position of its Cluster. A recording whose data is collected only at stop also starts with a SeekHead, which stores the positions of Info, Tracks, the first Cluster and the Cues. The page asks for data while it records, so its recordings carry Cues and no SeekHead. Each insertion, a Duration or the recipe's `Tags` below, moves every SeekPosition and CueClusterPosition at or past it by its length. A position that outgrows its field is written wider, which grows its SeekHead or Cues and moves what follows them; this repeats until no field grows, and a Segment of known size grows by everything added. The first SeekHead also gets a Seek entry for the recipe's `Tags`, so a reader that finds elements through it finds the recipe. A CRC-32 that opens an element covers the rest of that element's data. When the Duration is written, a CRC-32 opening Info is computed again. When an insertion rebuilds a SeekHead or Cues, a CRC-32 opening it or one of its parts is computed again over the rebuilt element. Each is stored little-endian, as EBML stores it. A file that also stores a Cluster Position, a CueCodecState or a CueReference, or has a CRC-32 that does not open its element or covers the whole Segment, is refused by name rather than saved with a stale value. Installed Edge 153 showed the same frames at each seek whether the Cues had moved or not, so a player there does not show this; the Node tests check the moved positions against the file's bytes.

The exporter copies each drawn frame onto a second canvas that nothing reads back, and hands the recorder that copy without its alpha plane (`alpha: 'discard'`), so translucent pixels arrive composited over black, as in the MP4. Edge's recorder writes a Colour element at the first frame and codes each frame by the backing of the canvas it records. A canvas on the GPU gives limited-range BT.709 (matrix, transfer and primaries 1, range 1); a canvas kept in memory gives full range (range 2, transfer 13). Three `getImageData` calls move a canvas to memory for good, so a piece that read its own canvas back from mid-film would switch the coding to full range under a Colour element that still says limited. Recording the copy keeps the whole film on the backing it started with. In installed Edge 153, on a colour chart of flat patches, recording the copy gave BT.709 limited range under a limited tag whether the piece read its canvas back never, from the first frame or from frame 11. Recording the drawing canvas itself, read back from frame 11, switched to full range there under the limited tag.

In headless Edge 153, drawing a `<video>` element onto a canvas converted a software-decoded (I420) frame's blue up to 12 levels off, while drawing a `VideoFrame` made from the same element was within 2 levels. Which decoder Edge used varied between films whose frames were byte-identical. Read a film's levels through a `VideoFrame` or a decoder, not through `drawImage` of the element.

A timeline with one frame still requires exactly one recorded frame, but has no spacing interval to validate. Its report uses zero for the gap statistics and `1 / hz` seconds for the frame's declared interval. This reported duration does not independently measure playback duration in a video player.

`window.__artifex.video()` returns the export report and blob without saving a file, whichever film the page offers. The WebM report carries `flashes` and `flashMs` as the MP4's does, counted on the frames the recorder is handed, at the design size, and the page's note adds the same warning. Where the browser has workers, each second's read back and count run in the worker, so they do not delay the frame the recorder is waiting for. The page's video button downloads that result. A still has no video timeline and cannot use this exporter.

The Node page tests cover both offers with stand-in encoders, and the WebM export's frames, saved duration, encoder warm-up and the 10-second limit on each request for data and on each stop with a stand-in recorder. Recordings built in both of Edge's shapes, and one with Cues before its first Cluster, check the moved positions, the Seek entry for the `Tags` and the CRC-32s of Info, a SeekHead and Cues. `npm run browser` exercises the MP4 offer, because installed Edge encodes H.264. `npm run browser -- --force no-h264` refuses H.264 in the page before it loads, requires the WebM offer for that reason, and records one film whose saved file must decode, last `frames / hz` seconds in Edge's video element, and hold every frame, evenly spaced; a heavily loaded machine can make that recording fall behind its schedule, and the page then refuses it, so the run records again, at most three recordings in all, and names each refusal. A completed build or Node suite does not verify the browser's encoder, download behavior, or how the saved video looks. Validate those in the target browser using the saved file.

### Writing a WebM from encoded frames

`muxWebm({ codec, width, height, hz, frames, seed, manifest, colour })` in [`core/export.js`](../../core/export.js) writes a WebM from frames a `VideoEncoder` already coded as VP8 or VP9, without `MediaRecorder` and so without the wall clock. No export calls it yet: the page's WebM is still the recording described above. It is there for a script that encodes frames itself.

- `frames` are the encoder's chunks in order, each `{ data, key }`, with `data` a `Uint8Array`. The first must be a keyframe.
- Frame `i` plays at `Math.round(i * 1e6 / hz)` microseconds, from its index and the frame rate alone. A slow piece keeps every frame in place.
- The Segment has a known size, Info states `frames / hz`, a SeekHead lists Info, Tracks and Cues, and Cues list every keyframe's Cluster, so a player can seek.
- The same input writes the same bytes. The track UID comes from `seed`, and no date is written.
- `manifest`, optional, goes in as the replay manifest, so `webmManifest` reads it back. `colour`, optional, is the encoder's colour space as WebCodecs names it.
- A codec other than `vp8` or `vp9`, a film with no frames or one that starts on a delta frame, a missing seed, a colour value with no WebM number and a `fullRange` that is not true or false are each refused by name.

The Node tests check the frame times, the index positions against the file's bytes, the length, the repeatable bytes and each refusal, on stand-in frames. Frames coded by ffmpeg 9.0.2 (VP9 at 30 and 29.97 Hz, VP8 at 24 Hz), written by `muxWebm` and read by `ffprobe`, gave every packet at its expected microsecond, the stated length, every keyframe flagged and every frame decoded with no error. No browser has played a file it wrote.

## Inspection and reproducibility

The page exposes `window.__artifex` for selecting examples, reading current state, inspecting named build stages, and retrieving replay metadata. `inspect(stage)` returns a summary instead of copying raw typed arrays or geometry.

The replay manifest records the library version, piece name, seed, parameters, dimensions and declared outputs, and, for a film whose box changes as it plays, its box timeline as `reframe`. For a piece that declares [images](../apis/piece-api.md#images), it records each one under `images`: its size, channels, source, rights and a digest of its pixels, never the pixels themselves. It contains neither the timeline definition nor a copy of the piece source. Replaying it requires the corresponding code, input data, and execution environment. These carry it:

| Carrier | Where | Adds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The page's `manifest()` | its return value | the quantized playhead `t` |
| An SVG, from `renderVector` or the page's SVG button | a `<metadata id="artifex-manifest">` element before the drawing, holding the manifest as JSON with `&`, `<`, `>` and `"` escaped | `t` |
| An MP4 film | a `uuid` box in `moov/udta` (see [the film's replay manifest](#the-films-replay-manifest)) | `film: { frames, hz, loop, scale }` |
| A PNG saved from the page, at any scale | an `iTXt` chunk before `IEND`, keyword `artifex-manifest`, no compression, empty language tag and translated keyword, holding the manifest as JSON | `t` and the export `scale` |
| A WebM film from the fallback | a `Tags` element before the first `Cluster`: one `Tag` with empty `Targets` (the whole film) and one `SimpleTag`, `TagName` `ARTIFEX_MANIFEST` and `TagString` holding the manifest as JSON | `film: { frames, hz, loop, scale: 1 }`, as the MP4 of the same film |

The WebM's tag is added to the saved file after its Duration. Every Cluster and Block keeps its bytes, and the stored positions past the tag move with them (see [WebM](#webm)). Players skip tags they do not use. `webmManifest(bytes)` in [`core/export.js`](../../core/export.js) reads it back, returns `null` for a WebM without one, and throws by name for a file that ends inside the element. `video()` also returns it as `report.manifest`.

The PNG's JSON is ASCII: any other character is written as a `\uXXXX` escape, as in a film, so it is also the UTF-8 that `iTXt` requires. The chunk is added to the bytes the browser encoded; removing it gives back those bytes exactly, and no pixel changes. Image tools that rewrite a PNG may drop it. `pngManifest(bytes)` in [`core/export.js`](../../core/export.js) reads it back, or returns `null` for a PNG without one. It reads only inside the chunk, and throws by name for a file that ends inside the chunk or a chunk without its text.

### Replaying a saved file

`npm run replay -- <file>` reads the manifest back from a saved SVG, PNG, MP4 film or WebM film and draws its recipe again. The piece is the registered example the manifest names, looked up as an own property, or the module passed with `--piece`; a module named inside the file is never loaded. A file made by another library version, for another piece, for a box or outputs the piece no longer declares, or with another image than the piece declares, is refused before anything is drawn; a piece that declares `boxes` is drawn again at the box the file names, if its ranges still hold it, and so is a PNG whose size, scale or playhead no longer matches, and a film whose frame grid (frame count, rate and loop), size or number of frames no longer matches. Every refusal and every mismatch exits nonzero and names the first difference.

An SVG is drawn again in Node and must be byte-identical; a difference is named by its first byte. `svgManifest(svg)` in [`core/surface-vector.js`](../../core/surface-vector.js) reads the recipe from any SVG, whether `renderVector` or the page's SVG button wrote it. The page draws its SVG with the browser's JavaScript engine, and replay draws it again with Node's. So for a page SVG, byte identity also means the two engines computed the same numbers, which ECMAScript does not promise (see [verification culture](verification-culture.md)). A differing byte cannot tell a changed recipe from a different engine. In installed Edge 153, the page's SVGs of settle, readout, cues, contours and inversion replayed byte-identical in Node 22.

A PNG is decoded in installed Edge and compared at full size, every channel and alpha included. It is compared with the still drawn again at its recipe, playhead and scale on a GPU canvas, as the page's PNG button draws, and with the frames on either side. It matches when every pixel is identical. Otherwise it must be within 40 dB PSNR of its redraw and at least as close to it as to either neighbour. In installed Edge 153, the page's PNGs of settle, drift and readout were identical to their redraws, and a PNG whose recipe named another seed, or whose playhead named the next frame, was refused.

An MP4 film is decoded in installed Edge, through the same driver as `npm run browser`, and compared at up to 24 frames spread evenly, first and last included. Each decoded frame must be within 30 dB PSNR of its redraw on a GPU canvas at the film's scale, compared at 240 pixels wide with drawn pixels over black, and at least as close to its own redraw as to either neighbour's; a held frame ties with an identical neighbour. In installed Edge, every example's own film matched its redraws, and a drift film drawn with another seed than its manifest names was refused.

A WebM is compared as an MP4 film is, with the same floor and rule. Each frame is sought midway through its own block, from the file's block times: the recorder stamps blocks by the wall clock, so a seek on the frame grid can land on a neighbour. Each decoded frame is read through a `VideoFrame`. In installed Edge 153, the page's WebMs of drift and readout matched, and a drift WebM whose recipe named the next seed was refused.

#### Comparing a film's soundtrack

An MP4 film's soundtrack is compared in the same page, after its pictures. A WebM carries no soundtrack, so replay compares its pictures only. The film and its piece must agree on whether there is one, and it must be AAC or Opus, the only codecs the export writes. The film's soundtrack is decoded as a player hears it, trimmed by its edit list, and must have the channels and the length, to the sample, of the recipe's soundtrack rendered and levelled as the export levels it for the film's codec (see [soundtrack loudness](#soundtrack-loudness)).

**The codec's floor.** Each block of 1024 samples must then be within 25 dB of the render for AAC, or 15 dB for Opus, unless the two differ by less than -60 dBFS, where the codec's own noise decides. Blocks are judged from their spectra without the first bin, 0 to 23 Hz, which holds the block's mean: it is not heard, and the Opus encoder removes it, so a Karplus-Strong pluck, whose noise burst leaves a mean in its string, lost it in its Opus film.

**Noise-like bands.** A perceptual codec keeps a noise's band levels but not its waveform, so a block under its floor is judged again by bands of 750 Hz. So is an AAC block that has noise-like bands and lies 25.0 dB or more and less than 26.0 dB from the render, judged on the unrounded figure, so a block printed as 26.0, such as 25.98, is still judged again, because the share of a short noise burst that a block holds moves its figure across the floor from one film to the next; an Opus block is judged again only under its floor. A band is noise-like where the render's own spectrum, through a Hann window, has a spectral flatness of 0.4 or more; the film never decides which bands are noise-like. The block still matches when both hold:

- the difference outside those bands keeps the codec's floor, measured against the whole block, and follows the rest of the render, outside those bands, within 10 dB of its own energy, unless that difference is quieter than -60 dBFS;
- inside them the film follows the render's waveform within 3 dB, over those bands together and in each band under 14 kHz that holds -25 dB of its block or more on its own, and its band levels within 18 dB, the level error summed as (√decoded − √rendered)² over the bands' energies.

Another noise (about -3 dB), a missing voice or one moved to another band (about 0 dB) misses the waveform; a voice louder or quieter misses the levels. A tone moved under loud noise (about -3 dB), missing (0 dB) or 6 dB quieter (6 dB) misses its own 10 dB, where the whole block could keep the Opus floor. Opus codes everything from 15.6 kHz up as one band, whose waveform it keeps loosely, and an Opus film of bright sine grains over near-silence decoded its 14.25 to 15 kHz band at 2.7 dB, so bands above 14 kHz, and bands holding less than -25 dB of their block, are judged only with the others. The first block outside is named by its time, with the noise-band measure it missed.

In installed Edge 153, films of two small pieces of filtered-noise voices and a quiet tone, one with every block under the AAC floor, replayed as matches as AAC and as Opus. Their films with the noise from another seed, missing, in another band or one voice 3 or 6 dB louder or quieter, or with the tone moved, all differed.

**Tones under a noise bed.** A codec codes tones coarsely where a noise in the same band masks them. Short tonal grains over a quiet noise bed, such as an `ambience` with `grains`, make their bands too tonal to count as noise-like. So replay applies a rule to a block that is under its floor. A band that is not noise-like is left out of the floor when the film's difference in that band lies under the render's own noise floor there. The margin is 6 dB or more for an AAC film and 3 dB or more for an Opus film. The band's noise floor is its median Hann-windowed bin, counted over all the bins of the band. The rest of the block must still keep the codec's floor and follow its own waveform within 10 dB. A verdict that used this rule names how many blocks needed it. The Opus margin was measured on films of grains far louder than their bed (see below). A WebM film carries no soundtrack, so this rule never applies to one. The AAC margin was measured in installed Edge 154 on AAC films of an ambience alone. Its grains peaked at 0.05 over a noise bed at 0.004 or 0.012. They rang for 0.03, 0.08 or 0.15 s, plain or chirping. Some blocks of these films were under the 25 dB floor. In each such band the film's difference lay 9.6 dB or more under the noise floor. All of these films replayed as matches. The same renders were also judged against wrong soundtracks. These were films moved by one sample, 1 ms or one block, films silenced or halved, renders without the bed, and renders with the grains rising over 10 ms. All of them differed. Without the rule, the floor alone would have let one wrong soundtrack pass only if bands 3.6 dB under the noise floor were also left out. That is why the margin is 6 dB, between 3.6 dB and 9.6 dB. The Opus films of the same pieces replayed within the 15 dB floor without the rule. Their worst blocks were at 15.1 to 16.3 dB. The same wrong soundtracks also differed as Opus.

**Grains far louder than their bed.** Replay can refuse a film made from its own recipe. This happens when an ambience's grains peak at 50 or more times its bed's level as AAC, or at 71 or more times as Opus. The export levels the whole soundtrack, so the ratio between the two levels decides, not the grain level alone. Grains in a band centred at 8 kHz can also be refused. To keep a grain ambience replayable on both codecs, keep two things. Keep the bed's level at 1/38 of the grains' peak or more, which is the widest ratio that matched on both codecs below. Keep the ambience's `colour` under 8000 Hz. A `colour` of 400 to 4000 Hz matched as Opus with the grains 12.5 times their bed. It was not measured as AAC at that ratio.

These measurements were made in installed Edge 154. Each film was 8 seconds of an ambience at the export's 192 kbit/s. The grains peaked at 0.05. Their rate rose from 8 to 120 grains a second and fell to 15. They rang for 0.08 s in a band 1.5 octaves wide. The bed was at 0.004. A case below can change any of these. These cases matched as Opus:

- Rings of 0.01 to 0.6 s.
- Chirps of -1 to 2 octaves.
- A `colour` of 400 to 4000 Hz.
- 400 grains a second.
- Every grain at one level.
- A low voice of partials under the grains.
- A quiet 1400 Hz tone under the grains.
- The same grains with no bed.
- Beds at 1/25 to 1/38 of the grain peak. These matched as AAC too.

These were refused:

| Grains to bed | Opus films | AAC films |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 times | none of 10 refused; 3 matched only by the rule for tones under a noise bed | 8 of 9 refused, by the floor or the noise-like bands' levels |
| 71 or 100 times | refused, by the noise-like bands' waveform or levels | refused, by the floor |
| 12.5 times, or 25 times with 400 chirping grains a second, `colour` 8000 Hz | refused: a noise-like band between 9.75 and 13.5 kHz followed the render's waveform under 3 dB | refused, by the floor |

These films set the Opus margin of the rule for tones under a noise bed. At 50 times, three Opus films kept the 15 dB floor only if the rule left out some bands. The difference in those bands lay 6.2, 4.6 and 4.6 dB under the noise floor. So Opus leaves out bands 3 dB under it.

Wrong soundtracks were also tested against the Opus films. The same renders were judged against Opus films, and so was the render of grains 12.5 times their bed. These films were moved by one sample, 1 ms or one block, or silenced or halved. The renders were also judged against the film of the grains rising slowly. All of these differed. The floor alone would have let one of them pass only if the rule also left out bands whose difference lay 6.6 dB over the noise floor.

The rule does not cover two groups of films. The first is the AAC films at 50 times. Either they would have needed the rule to leave out bands 5.2 to 2.0 dB under the noise floor, or another test refused them as well. The second is the films at 71 times or more on either codec. So replay can refuse a film like these because of the comparison, not because the recipe changed.

**Sparse noise contacts.** AAC codes noise a few milliseconds long, such as the `snap` contacts of the site's seams, more coarsely than a steady noise, and Opus does too. Replay allows for this in two ways, in an AAC or an Opus film.

- **Near bands.** The spectrum of a short burst rests on few independent values, so a band of plain noise can measure a flatness under 0.4 by chance and be judged as a tone. A band with a flatness of 0.2 or more counts as noise-like too when every judged band next to it is noise-like. A near band below 14 kHz that holds -25 dB of its block or more is also judged on its own, even beside an onset: the film must follow its waveform within 3 dB, and keep its level within 12 dB, the level error summed as for the noise-like bands. A quieter near band is judged only with the other noise-like bands, as a quiet noise-like band is, because AAC codes a band that quiet loosely: in a film of `stride`, a near band 35 dB under its block followed its waveform at -0.1 dB. A tone 2 to 4 times as loud as the noise in its band has a flatness in that range. Moved or dropped, it misses the waveform; 6 dB quieter, it misses the level.
- **Beside an onset.** Around an onset, AAC codes in short windows, which are coarser in frequency, and spreads its noise over its window, including into quiet top bands after a sound stops. A block is beside an onset when the render's loudest eighth of a block (128 samples), in that block or in a block next to it, is 6 dB or more louder than the block's average eighth. In such a block the noise-like bands are judged only together, near bands still on their own, and their levels are judged only below 14 kHz, within 12 dB instead of 18 dB. A verdict that used this rule names how many blocks were beside an onset and the worst level among them, and how many of them had no noise-like band below 14 kHz, so that no level was judged there.

In installed Edge 154, 14 of the 21 AAC films of the seven early seams at seeds 1 to 3 differed from their recipes without these two rules, and all 21 matched with them. Near bands with a flatness down to 0.24 decoded at 6.2 dB or better by waveform below 15 kHz, where the limit then stood, and those judged on their own at 13.8 dB or better by level. The five AAC films that the browser check exports, of readout, cues, settle, cascade and stride, matched as well, stride's at its own seed 4 once its near band 35 dB under its block was left to the other bands: 26 films in all. Beside an onset, single bands decoded as low as 1.0 dB by waveform, and the levels at 17.8 dB or better below 15 kHz, but 11.8 dB with the bands above 15 kHz included. The seed-1 films of crack, portal, trace and fold were also judged against 19 changed recipes: another seed, and a voice 3 dB quieter, dropped, 5 ms late or 12% higher. Every change that the earlier rules caught, outside the blocks where they also refused the unchanged film, still differed. Snaps 12% higher in portal and fold, and portal's glints 3 dB quieter, matched under both the earlier and the current rules: those changes lie within the codec's noise.

**Spill beside a louder block.** A codec codes each block in a window that spans the blocks on either side of it. A loud event therefore spreads its coding noise into a quiet block beside it, such as the faint end of a grain that rang out in the block before, where nothing masks that noise. A block that every rule above refuses still matches when it lies next to a block whose render is 12 dB or more louder than its own render, and its difference from the render lies far enough under that louder block's render: 30 dB for AAC, 25 dB for Opus, which spreads more. Both neighbours are considered, and the louder one counts. A verdict that used the rule names how many blocks needed it.

In installed Edge 154, 4 of 23 AAC films of `core/sound.js` ambience grains 5 to 80 ms long, with no bed, at seeds 1 to 6, differed without the rule. Each differed in one block, right after a grain's loudest block. That block decoded from -0.1 to 22.2 dB from the render. It was 15.2 to 41.9 dB louder than its own render, and 35.7 to 44.7 dB over its difference. With the rule, those films matched. Their recipes with another seed, or with the grains 1 ms late, still differed.

In installed Edge 155, the Opus films of `tests/fixtures/grain-film.cjs` at seeds 1 to 6 each differed without the sparse-noise rules. With them, 10 blocks still spilled, 28.2 to 46.7 dB under their louder neighbour by their difference, so Opus takes 25 dB. With both, all six matched. The Opus films of `tests/fixtures/burst-film.cjs` at seeds 1 to 3 matched with and without them. Those nine films still differed from changed recipes: another seed, moved by a sample, 1 ms or a block, or 3 dB quieter or louder.

**The band the codec kept.** A codec also cuts the top of the spectrum, so each block is judged only below the band the film's codec kept, measured over the whole film from each bin's Hann-windowed power in the render and in the film. Scanning down from the top, the cut is a run of bins the film holds 10 dB or more under the render, then at most one band of transition, then a bin within 2 dB of the render; bins the render holds 60 dB under its loudest are no evidence either way. It is taken only when that edge lies above 12 kHz, and every band from the one that holds the edge upwards goes unjudged; the verdict then names the frequency it judged below, and the report carries it as `sound.cut` in Hz. A film missing a voice, or low-passed gently or under 12 kHz, has no such edge and is judged over every band.

**Measured on the noise fixture.** `tests/fixtures/noise-film.cjs`, a public fixture, sounds eighth-note hi-hats high-passed at 7 kHz over a bass and a chord, a breath and a fricative-like burst high-passed at 1 and 4 kHz crossfading with a voiced line, Karplus-Strong plucks, a pop high-passed at 1.5 kHz, a hiss high-passed at 5 kHz, and a tone cut to silence in 30 ms. In installed Edge 153, its AAC film lost the render from about 18.6 kHz up and its Opus film from about 20.1 kHz. Judged over every band and with its mean, both films missed the rule above in some blocks, by their sound above the cut or the plucks' mean. Judged below the band each codec kept, both matched. Replayed against the same fixture with one of its voices from another seed, louder, quieter, missing or moved, both films differed. One case later passed that should not have: the fixture's Opus film replayed against the fixture with its hi-hats 3 dB quieter matched, its worst block 15.8 dB from the render, over Opus's 15 dB floor, so the block was judged by the floor alone. An Opus block with noise-like bands within 1 dB over its floor is now judged by the noise rules too, as an AAC block is, and that film differs there, its noise-like bands keeping the render's levels at 8.4 dB where 12 are asked. With that rule the fixture's own Opus film still matches, and so do the Opus films of readout, cues, settle, cascade and stride, of the drum and speech fixtures, and of `tests/fixtures/grain-film.cjs` at seeds 1 to 6; the hi-hats 3 dB louder, or from another seed, differed before and after.

**Measured on the examples and the limited fixtures.** In installed Edge 153, the films of readout, cues and settle and of the limited fixtures `tests/fixtures/drum-film.cjs` and `tests/fixtures/speech-film.cjs` replayed as matches, AAC and Opus; the drum film's AAC blocks sit at the 25 dB floor. Settle's soundtrack is short events: edge snaps, knocks and plucks. Each snap and knock is as loud as the logarithm of its speed over the range its own kind spans, and each pluck as loud as the distance its node flies. In installed Edge 154 its film replayed as a match again, every AAC block within 31.2 dB of the render and every Opus block within 23.3 dB. With each band and the rest judged on their own, they still matched, and so did variants of the noise fixture with a quieter noise voice or a tone under a broad noise. The same variants with the quieter voice from another seed, or the tone moved, all differed.

**Changed recipes.** A film whose piece drew the same pictures with its tone moved differed at its first block, and the same piece's film without a soundtrack was named as carrying none. The next seed's soundtrack of readout and settle differed from their films; settle's soundtrack, whose events the seed moves to other frames: in installed Edge 154, its AAC film with its manifest's seed changed to the next one decoded 5.7 dB from that seed's render at its first block. Cues' soundtrack barely follows the seed, so its next seed passes as Opus.

#### Replaying a joined film

A joined film from `npm run stitch` is replayed scene by scene against the recipe that scene records (see [joining scene films](#joining-scene-films)). The scenes of registered examples share one Edge page, and the scenes of each external piece share another. Each page receives the film once and compares its scenes in turn; `--timeout-ms` sets the time allowed for each scene in it. A scene's piece is the registered example of its name, or the module passed with `--piece` whose piece has that name; pass `--piece` once for each external piece. Each scene is checked as a film of its own: its frame grid and size against its piece, and up to 24 of its frames, its first and last included, so the frames on both sides of every join are compared. Its sound is judged from `sound.at` in the decoded soundtrack, over the samples `sound.judged` names; the verdict says which seconds of the scene were judged. The joined soundtrack must decode to exactly the film's length. The film matches when every scene matches; otherwise the verdict names the first scene that differs, and each scene gets its own line and report entry.

#### What a match does not show

A recipe that draws nearly the same pictures, such as a small parameter change, can pass a film comparison within that tolerance, and a soundtrack change smaller than its codec's noise passes the soundtrack comparison, as does a change inside noise-like bands that keeps their waveform within 3 dB and their levels within 18 dB, and any change above the band the film's codec kept. A noise voice replaced by another still passes where it shares its bands with louder noise, sits above 14 kHz, or holds less than -25 dB of its block in every band: in `tests/replay.test.js`, a hi-hat drawn from another seed differs 8 to 14 dB under the other noise voices of its blocks and matches 18 dB under, and a quiet noise 41 dB under its block, or one above 14 kHz, from another seed, matches. Beside an onset, no noise-like band is judged on its own unless it is a near band. In an AAC or Opus film, that leaves any noise voice below 14 kHz open to a swap, however loud it is: within one block of a transient 6 dB or more over its block's average eighth, which any voice can cause, the voice replaced by another passes when the block's noise-like bands together still keep their waveform within 3 dB and their levels below 14 kHz within 12 dB. A quiet noise voice under a louder contact does. A tone about as loud as the noise in its near band can pass 3 dB quieter: in a synthetic film of such a tone over a noise voice, it did. In a near band holding less than -25 dB of its block, a tone moved, dropped or 6 dB quieter can pass, because that band is judged only with the others. A change confined to a quiet block beside a block 12 dB or more louder passes when its difference lies 30 dB (AAC) or 25 dB (Opus) or more under that louder block, whatever the block holds. The rule checks the size of the difference, not its cause, so it cannot tell the codec's spilled noise from a changed recipe. An SVG rendered with a `background` option replays without it and differs, because the manifest does not record it.

One honest film is still refused. Sparse bright sine pings over near-silence, with nothing else sounding, differ from their own recipe as AAC: in installed Edge 157 a block beside an onset decoded 21.2 dB from the render, under the 25 dB floor, with its levels at 10.0 dB, under 12 dB. The same piece as Opus matches since the limit moved to 14 kHz, and both codecs match once a quiet bed sounds under the pings.
